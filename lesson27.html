Lesson 27

Critical Reading and Analysis

← Back to Lessons
🎯 Learning Objectives

At the end of the lesson, learners should be able to:

  • Define critical reading and critical analysis.
  • Distinguish between claims and evidence in a text.
  • Recognize underlying assumptions in arguments.
  • Evaluate the validity and reliability of arguments.
  • Explain the importance of critical reading in everyday life and academics.
💡 Introduction & Discussion

Critical reading requires looking beyond the literal meaning of words to consider the quality, validity, and logic of a message.

1. Critical Analysis

Critical analysis is the process of examining different parts of a text to understand how ideas are developed and supported. It includes examining main ideas, supporting evidence, arguments, word choice, purpose, and possible bias.

2. Claims and Evidence

• Claim: A statement or position that a writer wants readers to accept.
Example Claim: Reading regularly improves vocabulary.

• Evidence: Facts, examples, research findings, or information used to support a claim.
Example Evidence: A study showing that students who read frequently learn more unfamiliar words.

3. Assumptions

An assumption is an idea accepted as true without sufficient proof or examination (e.g., assuming every student using a smartphone spends all their time playing games).

4. Evaluating Arguments

When evaluating arguments, readers should ask:

  • Is the claim clear?
  • Is there enough evidence?
  • Are the sources reliable?
  • Does the reasoning make sense?
  • Are there possible biases or missing details?

5. Importance of Critical Reading

Helps readers understand complex messages, identify reliable info, recognize weak arguments, avoid misinformation, and make informed decisions.

📌 Practical Examples

Example 1: Claim

"Students should have access to a school library because reading materials support learning."

Presents a writer's position.

Example 2: Evidence

"According to the school's records, library visits increased during the reading program."

Provides data to support an argument.

Example 3: Weak Assumption

"Everyone who disagrees with the policy does not care about education."

Unfair generalization and unsupported assumption.

Example 4: Critical Reading

Checking author qualifications and examining evidence before accepting claims.

Active evaluation of information.

🔑 Key Takeaways

  • Critical reading involves questioning and evaluating written information.
  • Critical analysis examines how ideas are presented and supported.
  • Claims express a writer's position, while evidence supports the claims.
  • Assumptions are ideas accepted without sufficient proof.
  • Evaluating arguments helps readers recognize reliable and misleading information.
