Lesson 29

Understanding Informational and Argumentative Texts

← Back to Lessons
🎯 Learning Objectives

At the end of the lesson, learners should be able to:

  • Define informational and argumentative texts.
  • Identify the characteristics and parts of both text types.
  • Distinguish between informational and argumentative writing.
  • Evaluate claims, reasons, counterarguments, and rebuttals.
  • Explain the importance of recognizing different text types in reading.
💡 Introduction & Discussion

1. Informational Text

An informational text provides facts, definitions, and logical explanations about a topic to inform or explain rather than persuade readers.

Characteristics: Factual data, clear explanations, logical organization, specific subject focus.
Examples: News reports, textbooks, encyclopedias, informational articles.

2. Argumentative Text

An argumentative text presents a central claim or position and supports it using reasons and solid evidence to convince readers that the stance is reasonable.

3. Key Parts of an Argumentative Text

  • Claim: The main position or statement the writer wants readers to accept.
  • Reasons: Explanations showing why the writer supports the claim.
  • Evidence: Facts, statistics, examples, or research validating the reasons.
  • Counterargument: An opposing viewpoint that challenges the main claim.
  • Rebuttal: A response explaining why the writer's position remains valid despite the counterargument.
  • Conclusion: The final section summarizing the argument and reinforcing the position.

4. Informational vs. Argumentative Texts

• Informational texts aim to increase knowledge through facts.
• Argumentative texts aim to convince readers to accept a stance using reasons and evidence.

📌 Summary Examples

Example 1: Informational Text

"Plants need sunlight, water, and nutrients to grow."

Provides factual knowledge about plant development.

Example 2: Argumentative Text

"Schools should establish more reading programs because they help students develop reading skills."

Presents a position supported by a clear reason.

Example 3: Counterargument

"Some people believe that online learning is more convenient. However, others argue that face-to-face interaction is essential."

Presents opposing perspectives on an issue.

Example 4: Argumentative Text

"Although printed books are useful, digital books should also be available because they offer convenient access."

Defends a recommendation using logical reasoning.

🔑 Key Takeaways

  • Informational texts provide facts and explanations about a topic.
  • Argumentative texts present a claim supported by reasons and evidence.
  • Claims, reasons, and evidence are core building blocks of arguments.
  • Counterarguments acknowledge opposing viewpoints, while rebuttals counter them.
  • Recognizing text types helps readers evaluate the writer's primary purpose.
