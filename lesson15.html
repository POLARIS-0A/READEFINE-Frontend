Level 2 • Lesson 15

Making Inferences

← Back to Lessons
🎯 Learning Objectives

At the end of this lesson, learners should be able to:

  • Define what an inference is.
  • Identify textual clues and prior knowledge.
  • Distinguish between explicit information and inferences.
  • Make reasonable inferences about characters and situations.
💡 Introduction & Discussion

Sometimes, authors do not directly explain everything in a story or text. They leave clues, and readers use these clues together with what they already know to understand something that is not directly explained.

1. What Is an Inference?

An inference is a reasonable conclusion that readers make by combining textual clues with what they already know (prior knowledge).

Sentence: Mark entered the house with wet clothes and an umbrella.
🎯 Inference: It was probably raining outside.
The sentence does not directly say that it was raining, but the details provide clues.

2. Textual Clues

Textual clues are words, statements, and details in a text that help readers understand information and make inferences. These clues may describe actions, feelings, situations, or events.

Sentence: Lisa looked at the clock repeatedly and kept checking the door.
Clues: Repeatedly looked at the clock, kept checking the door.
🎯 Possible Inference: Lisa may be waiting for someone or something.

3. Prior Knowledge

Prior knowledge refers to information, experiences, and understanding that readers already have before reading a text. Readers use prior knowledge together with textual clues to make reasonable inferences.

Text: The children wore thick jackets, gloves, and scarves.
Prior Knowledge: These clothes are commonly used in cold weather.
🎯 Inference: The children are probably in a cold place.

4. Inference and Explicit Information

Explicit information is directly stated in the text, whereas an inference is a conclusion that readers make based on clues and prior knowledge.

Text: "John carried a birthday cake into the room. His friends shouted, 'Surprise!'"
• Explicit Information: John carried a birthday cake; his friends shouted "Surprise!"
• Inference: The friends prepared a surprise celebration.

5. Making Inferences About Characters and Situations

Readers can make inferences about characters by examining their actions, words, expressions, and reactions, as well as understand situations that are not directly explained.

Example (Character): Mia smiled widely when she saw the high score on her examination. ➡️ Mia was probably happy or proud.
Example (Situation): The streets were wet, people were carrying umbrellas, and cars moved slowly. ➡️ It had probably rained recently.

6. How to Make a Reasonable Inference

  • Read the text carefully.
  • Identify important words and details.
  • Use prior knowledge.
  • Connect the clues with what is already known.
  • Make a conclusion that is supported by the information.
📌 Practical Examples

Example 1: Inferring Feelings

Anna received a gift and immediately smiled while thanking her friend.

Inference: Anna was happy or grateful.

Example 2: Inferring Weather

People were holding umbrellas while walking along the street.

Inference: It may be raining.

Example 3: Inferring a Situation

The classroom was empty, the lights were off, and the chairs were arranged neatly.

Inference: Classes may have already ended or the room is not currently being used.

🔑 Key Takeaways

  • An inference is a reasonable conclusion based on clues and prior knowledge.
  • Textual clues are details that help readers understand information.
  • Prior knowledge is what readers already know before reading.
  • Explicit information is directly stated in a text.
  • Inferences are not directly stated but are supported by clues.
