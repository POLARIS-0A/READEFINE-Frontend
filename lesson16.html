Level 2 • Lesson 16

Understanding Text Structure

← Back to Lessons
🎯 Learning Objectives

At the end of this lesson, learners should be able to:

  • 1. Define text structure.
  • 2. Identify common types of text structure.
  • 3. Recognize signal words that indicate different text structures.
  • 4. Explain how text structure helps readers understand information.
💡 Introduction & Discussion

Writers organize information in different ways depending on the purpose of their writing. Some texts explain how events happen, while others compare ideas, describe problems, or present solutions.
Text structure refers to the way information and ideas are organized in a written text.
Understanding text structure helps readers recognize how ideas are connected and how the author presents information.

1. What Is Text Structure?

Text structure is the organization or arrangement of information and ideas in a written text. It helps writers present information clearly and helps readers follow the message.

Example: A recipe presents instructions in a sequence, while a comparison article organizes information by discussing similarities and differences.

2. Description

Description is a text structure that explains the characteristics, features, or qualities of a person, place, object, or idea.

Signal Words: For example, such as, including, specifically, characteristics include.

Example: The school library is a quiet and organized place. It contains shelves of books, reading tables, computers, and study areas.

3. Sequence or Chronological Order

This structure presents events or steps in the order they happen. It is commonly used in stories, historical accounts, and instructions.

Signal Words: First, next, then, after, before, finally.

Example: First, prepare the materials. Next, follow the instructions. Finally, check your work.

4. Compare and Contrast

Explains the similarities (compare) and differences (contrast) between two or more subjects.

Signal Words: Similarly, both, likewise, however, unlike, in contrast, on the other hand.

Example: Books and e-books are both sources of reading material. However, printed books use paper, while e-books are read using electronic devices.

5. Cause and Effect

Explains why something happens (cause) and what happens as a result (effect).

Signal Words: Because, since, therefore, as a result, consequently, due to.

Example: It rained heavily throughout the night. As a result, some streets became flooded.
• Cause: Heavy rainfall.
• Effect: Some streets became flooded.

6. Problem and Solution

Presents an issue (problem) and explains one or more ways to address it (solution).

Signal Words: Problem, issue, solution, to solve this, one way, to address the problem.

• Problem: Students often forget their assignment deadlines.
• Solution: They can use a planner or calendar to organize their tasks.

7. How to Identify Text Structure

  • Read the entire passage carefully.
  • Identify the main topic.
  • Observe how the information is organized.
  • Look for signal words.
  • Determine whether the author is describing, sequencing, comparing, explaining causes/effects, or presenting a problem/solution.
📌 Summary Examples

Example 1: Description

"The beach has soft sand, clear water, and tall coconut trees."

Reason: Explains the characteristics of a beach.

Example 2: Compare & Contrast

"Both laptops and tablets can be used for studying. However, laptops have keyboards..."

Reason: Presents similarities and differences.

Example 3: Cause & Effect

"The students practiced regularly; therefore, their performance improved."

Cause: Regular practice | Effect: Improved performance

🔑 Key Takeaways

  • Text structure is the way information is organized in a text.
  • Description explains characteristics of a subject.
  • Sequence presents events or steps in order.
  • Compare and contrast identifies similarities and differences.
  • Cause and effect explains reasons and results.
  • Problem and solution presents an issue and ways to address it.
  • Signal words help readers recognize text structures.
